# M2 · Ground truth

**Scale:** atmosphere — §2.8's lifted-print regime, and the vacuum it fades out of.
**Modules:** `terrain.js`, `surface.js`, new `material.js`, new `paint.js`, new `print.js`
**Flag:** `?m2=1`, default-off (§7.4). Flipping the default is a separate commit.

§M2 asks for ground you can name from a still: four-layer triplanar materials,
a depth-graded ocean, far ridges as pure silhouette, and — first, because they
change every subsequent frame — §9.2's light model and §9.3's aerial
perspective.

This plan puts one thing ahead of both of those, and §0 argues for it.

---

## 0 · Sequencing, and a deviation from §6's own ordering

§M2 says *"Port §9.2 (`paint()`) and §9.3 (aerial perspective) first; they change
every subsequent frame."* This plan ports **§9.4, the print**, ahead of them.

The argument is not that the print matters more. It is that the print is the
transform every later judgement is made through:

1. **§9.2's defining feature is a hue ramp with visibly banded edges.** §9.2
   says so, and §11 lists deleting them as the archetypal PBR reflex. ACES is a
   filmic curve whose entire purpose is to compress and desaturate exactly that
   structure. Porting the light model and then scoring it through ACES measures
   the tonemap, not the light model — and §8 demands one sentence naming the
   pixel region that lost the point, which is not answerable when the curve is
   the thing eating it.
2. **Everything downstream gets tuned against the viewing transform.** Land the
   print last and §9.2 and §9.3 are tuned twice, the second time against a
   frame that no longer looks like the one they were tuned for.
3. **The print is the one act that could be built without a GPU**, which is what
   this session had. It is fragment maths with an exact CPU reference (§7.3),
   and its correctness is decidable offline.

The cost, named so it cannot be quietly dropped: §9.4 steps 5 (watercolour
softening at `0.42 × fog`) and its chroma bleed both read the fog fraction from
the alpha channel, which is §9.3's aerial perspective and does not exist yet.
They are deferred to act 2 and land with the thing that writes a distance.

## 3 · Two rulings, one uniform

§3 makes two separate rulings, and it is easy to implement one and believe you
have implemented both.

| Row | Ruling | Rides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | OLED true-black vs. the reference's lift → **split by medium**; vacuum true `#000`, atmosphere lifted; cross-fade on entry | `uPaint` |
| 3 | AEON's `1 − exp(−1.32·c)` vs. the reference's rational curve → **the reference curve wins at surface scale; AEON's survives in vacuum**; cross-fade on entry | `uPaint` |

Row 1 is about the grade. Row 3 is about the **tonemap** — a different stage,
before the grade, and the first draft of this act got it wrong: it scaled every
graded step by `uPaint` and ran the reference's rational curve at all scales.

That is not a subtle difference. The two curves diverge hardest exactly where
the cosmic web lives:

| linear in | `1 − exp(−1.32·c)` | rational print | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.02 | 0.0261 | 0.0693 | 2.66× |
| 0.20 | 0.232 | 0.385 | 1.66× — widest absolute gap, 0.153 |
| 1.00 | 0.733 | 0.703 | 0.96× |

Measured on the frame rather than the curve: the cosmic web's mean luminance
went 0.204 through ACES → **0.381** through the rational curve. Nearly the whole
gain was the toe. With row 3 implemented, vacuum reads 0.124.

So `tonemap()` takes `uPaint` as well:

```glsl
vec3 tonemap(vec3 x, float paint) {
  x = max(x, vec3(0.0));
  return mix(tonemapVacuum(x), tonemapPrint(x), paint);
}
```

Both curves are monotone and both send 0 to 0, so the blend is monotone at every
`uPaint` and true black survives the whole descent rather than only its
endpoints. Both properties are asserted in `tools/verify.js`, not assumed.

### Where `uPaint` comes from

`paintForScale()` in `print.js`, called once per frame from `main.js` before
`post.render()`:

| scale | `uPaint` |
|---|---|
| cosmic, galaxy, system, blackhole | 0 |
| surface, clouds | 1 |
| planet | `clamp(1 − (alt − 0.0003)/0.35, 0, 1)` — full print by ~2 km, none above ~1 planet radius |

The planet scale is the interesting one because it spans both media: it begins
in orbit, which is vacuum, and ends on the ground, which is not. The same
descent that drives the hyperzoom drives the grade, which is what §3 asks for.

## 5 · Four deliberate deviations from the reference

Each one is forced by an invariant the reference does not have to satisfy,
because the reference is one valley in one atmosphere and AEON is six scales.

1. **The tonemap cross-fades.** The reference has one medium and one curve.
   §3 row 3. See §3 above.
2. **Paper tooth and vignette are scaled by `uPaint`.** The reference applies
   both unconditionally. A warm-dark vignette multiplies the frame toward
   `(0.62, 0.60, 0.66)`, which in vacuum is a tint over a field that should be
   black, and paper tooth on a true-black field is a lift with extra steps.
   §2.8.
3. **The dither is gated on luma.** §9.4 step 8 is a flat ±0.5/255; applied at
   zero it rounds half the vacuum to 1/255. The gate is
   `smoothstep(0, 1.5/255, luma)`, so true black stays exactly black and the
   first visible step — which is where banding actually lives — is dithered at
   full strength. §2.8 against §9.4, and both survive. (This was already M1's
   resolution for the same collision; act 1 carries it into the print.)
4. **The grade pass gives up its vignette and its grain.** `post.js` has had a
   per-world grade since before either milestone: lift, gain, saturation,
   vignette, grain. Under M2 the last two belong to §9.4, which owns them and
   scales them by medium. Two vignettes and two grains in series is the
   double-grade §9.4's step order exists to prevent — and in vacuum the grade's
   grain was worse than redundant. See §6.

The resonance keeps lift, gain and saturation. Those are a world's colour
intent, not the print.

## 6 · One defect the new curve exposed

The grade pass adds `g · uGrain · min(3l + 0.08, 1)` in linear light, ungated by
medium, with a neutral default of `uGrain = 0.02`. The vacuum scales never set
`gradeSettings` — only `planetscale.js` and `surface.js` do — so the cosmic web
had been getting film grain and a 0.12 vignette all along, at a scale where
§2.8 says the background is true `#000`.

ACES concealed it. Its toe near zero is flat enough that a ±0.0008 floor rounds
back to 0 at 8 bits. AEON's own curve has roughly **12× the slope at the
origin** and prints the same floor as 1/255.

*The grain was always wrong. Only the tonemap that hid it has changed.* That
sentence is the reason this act was worth doing before the ones §M2 lists first:
the print is not just a look, it is the instrument that makes the other errors
measurable.

## 7 · The bloom pedestal, and why act 2 has to composite it

Removing the grade's grain did not restore black. Bisecting the chain at runtime
found the real source, and it is structural.

| configuration | frame at true `#000` |
|---|---|
| as shipped | 0.0% |
| bloom pass disabled entirely | **76.2%** |
| bloom strength 0 | 75.8% |
| grade pass disabled | 0.0% |

`UnrealBloomPass` composites five mip levels, the coarsest at 1/32 resolution
with a separable kernel that spans the frame. Every bright pixel therefore
contributes to *every* pixel. In an atmosphere that is veiling glare and it is
welcome; in vacuum it is a pedestal under a field that must reach zero.

No setting removes it. The full sweep, at the cosmic scale, seed 20250601,
a = 0.55, 640 × 400, `m1=1&slab=1&comp=1&q=desktop&m2=1`:

| knob | `#000` | lit | mean | peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| shipped — radius 0.75, threshold 0, strength 0.85 | 0.0% | 99.0% | 0.123 | 0.885 |
| radius 0.25 | 0.0% | 87.6% | 0.114 | 0.911 |
| radius 0 | 0.0% | 78.9% | 0.110 | 0.922 |
| threshold 0.15 | 30.4% | 22.3% | 0.023 | 0.765 |
| threshold 0.4 | 40.7% | 19.5% | 0.020 | 0.770 |
| strength 0.15 | 22.0% | 19.8% | 0.020 | 0.762 |
| bloom floor 0.0012 | 0.0% | 75.8% | 0.098 | 0.801 |
| bloom floor 0.005 | 28.9% | 42.8% | 0.053 | 0.778 |
| radius 0 + floor 0.003 | 36.4% | 43.6% | 0.068 | 0.843 |

Every row that recovers black deletes the web to do it. Nothing reaches the
76% that turning the pass off reaches. Zeroing `bloomFactors` for the coarse
mips does not help either, and is worth knowing why: `lerpBloomFactor` mixes
each factor toward `1.2 − factor`, so at radius 0.75 setting a factor to 0
*raises* that mip's weight to 0.9.

**The conclusion is that this pass cannot satisfy §2.8, at any setting.** The
fix is the reference's structure — a bright pass, a bounded blur chain, and the
composite performed *inside* the print (`c += bl · uBloomAmt`, reference
4421–4422) where it can be firewalled and where §9.4's step order puts it
anyway. That is act 2, and this measurement is the reason act 2 does it.

What act 1 ships instead is the mechanism, not the fix: `firewallBloom()`
patches the composite material — at runtime, so `/vendor` stays byte-verifiable
per §10 — to add §11's **first** NaN firewall, which was missing, and a
`uBloomFloor` knob driven by `?bfloor=`. The floor defaults to **0**, so act 1
does not touch the halo. Every replacement the patch makes is checked, and a
miss throws: a patch that silently fails to apply is precisely the defect
§M0's shader gate exists to catch.

## 10 · Two gaps in the instrument, closed

Neither is M2 work. Both were found by M2 work failing in ways the instrument
should have caught first.

**`tools/parse.js` — the parse gate.** A backtick inside a GLSL template
literal, in a *comment*, terminates the template and turns the rest of the
shader into JavaScript. This has now cost two runs. Every downstream tool
reports the same useless symptom — the page did not boot — because the module
never parsed. `node --check` is not the guard it looks like: given a file
containing an `import`, it detects ESM, declines to parse, and exits 0. Copying
to `.mjs` makes it check for real. It runs first in `tools/check.js`, ahead of
everything that launches a browser to discover the same thing more slowly.

**`tools/shadercheck.js --flags`.** The compile gate flew the bench route once,
unflagged. §7.4 says milestone work is built behind a default-off flag — so the
gate was compiling the build nobody is iterating on and reporting green while
every new shader in the repo went unchecked. It now flies the route once per
flag combination, defaulting to the shipped build plus `m1=1&m2=1&slab=1`.

## 16 · Act 2 · Aerial perspective, and the alpha channel

Designed here, per §7.2, ahead of the code. The CPU reference is already in
`tools/verify.js` (§7.3 puts it before the render loop, not after), and it is
green — 12 checks, §18.

### 16.1 · What is there now

`surface.js`, one line at the end of the terrain fragment shader:

```glsl
float dist = length(vW - uCam);
lit = mix(lit, uHorizon * max(dusk, 0.08), 1.0 - exp(-dist * 0.0007));
gl_FragColor = vec4(lit, 1.0);
```

`planetscale.js` is the same shape with a per-world coefficient:
`fog = 1 − exp(−dCam · uHazeK)`, `uHazeK = 0.012 · atmoAmt · hazeX ·
exp(−alt/(R·0.012))`, mixed toward a single `uHaze`.

Both are one colour, one exponent, no height falloff along the ray, no valley
mist — and both write `1.0` into alpha, so the fog fraction is computed and
thrown away on every pixel of every frame.

### 16.2 · The port

One shared GLSL chunk, called from every surface-scale fragment shader at the
point each currently does its own fog. The reference's `aerial()` writes its
fog fraction to a mutable global `gFogAmt` and returns only the colour; that is
a GLSL convenience rather than a design, and it makes the one value act 2 exists
to produce the easiest thing in the function to forget. AEON's returns `vec4`.

The convention that matters, and that the CPU suite now pins: **`V` points from
the surface toward the camera** — `normalize(uCamPos - P)`, which is what the
reference uses at all ten of its call sites. Reversed, the Mie term inverts and
the fog goes *cold* toward the sun. That still looks like fog. It is the wrong
image, and nothing in a still would tell you.

### 16.3 · Three things that cannot port as literals

The reference is one valley under one star. Each of its constants is a
measurement of that place, and §2 says the physics is never negotiable.

**a · `fogNear 70` / `fogFar 1700` are extinction lengths.** They are a property
of the air, not of the world's size — so they should not scale with AEON's
1400 m surface extent, they should scale with how much air there is.
AEON already carries that: `atmoAmt · hazeX` in `planetscale.js`.

> **Recommendation:** `fogFar = 1700 / (atmoAmt · hazeX)`, `fogNear` likewise,
> so the *shape* of the curve is the reference's and the *thickness* is the
> world's. An airless world takes `atmoAmt → 0`, `fogFar → ∞`, and the fog
> vanishes rather than needing a special case — which is the check that this is
> the right parameterisation rather than a fitted one.

**b · The air colours must come from the star.** `K_HAZE`, `K_MIST`,
`K_SKY_HORSUN` and `K_SKY_ANTI` are four hex literals in the reference. §9.6
already ruled on this for the sky and the ruling covers the air: *"derive the
four sky stops from the star's spectrum through a fixed transfer, rather than
hardcoding them. The stops above are that transfer's output for a G-type star at
13.5°. That is the port: not the values, the function that produced them."*

> **Recommendation:** act 2 ports the transfer, and the reference's four values
> become the **G-type fixture** the CPU suite pins it against — if the transfer
> does not reproduce them for a 5778 K star at 13.5°, the transfer is wrong.
> That turns §9.6's ruling into a test rather than an intention.

**c · The `260 m` height falloff is a haze scale height,** not an atmospheric
one. Earth's atmospheric scale height is ≈ 8.5 km; 260 m is the shallow
boundary-layer haze that pools in a valley, which is exactly what the term is
for.

> **Recommendation:** `H_haze = 0.0306 · H_atm`, with the coefficient set *by*
> the reference rather than guessed — 260/8500 — and `H_atm = kT/(mg)` from the
> world's own numbers, which `planetscale.js` has. Same discipline as (a): the
> reference supplies the fixture, physics supplies the scaling.

### 16.4 · Alpha, and why the bloom has to move

§9.3's trick is that the fog fraction goes in the alpha channel so the post
chain knows each pixel's distance. §9.4 step 5 then spends it on
distance-graded watercolour softening and chroma bleed. Act 1 deferred both for
exactly this.

The hazard is that alpha has to *survive the composer*. The grade pass already
passes it through (`gl_FragColor = vec4(max(col, 0.0), c.a)`). The bloom pass
does not: `UnrealBloomPass` blends its result with `AdditiveBlending` and
`transparent: true`, so it adds the bloom's alpha to the scene's. Every pixel's
"distance" would then be its distance *plus how much it glows*.

The reference has no such problem because it never runs bloom as a pass — it
renders the bloom chain to its own target and composites it **inside the
print**: `c += bl · uBloomAmt · mix(vec3(1.0), K_SUN, 0.55)` (line 4421).

So the two halves of act 2 are one change:

1. Bloom stops being a composer pass and becomes a texture the print samples.
2. Alpha survives untouched from the scene to the print, carrying the fog.

And it resolves §7 as a side effect. A bright pass plus a bounded blur chain has
**compact support** — a pixel far from any light receives nothing — where the
mip pyramid's coarsest level covers the frame by construction. That is the
structural reason no setting of `UnrealBloomPass` reaches §2.8's true black, and
the structural reason this one will. §11's two firewalls land at the bright pass
and in the print, where the section says to put them.

## 19 · Act 2 step 2 · The star→air-colour transfer

`src/starlight.js`, verified by `node tools/verify.js starlight` — 18 checks.

§9.6 makes two demands that pull against each other. The sky is *"a painted
gradient, not a scattering integral"* — and the painted values must come out of
*"a fixed transfer"* rather than a table, because AEON has 10²⁸ stars and the
reference has one. A scattering integral satisfies the second and loses the
first. A lookup table does the reverse.

**The transfer does both by moving the painted values by the chromatic
difference between two stars.** Compute the air's light under star *T* from
first principles; compute it for the fixture, a 5778 K sun at 13.5°;
chromatically adapt §9.1's painted stop from the fixture's white to *T*'s, in
Bradford LMS. At the fixture the adaptation is the identity and the painted
values emerge untouched — by construction, not by fitting.

### What is computed rather than chosen

Planck's law · the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions, as Wyman, Sloan &
Shirley's analytic multi-lobe fit so there is no data file (§2.1) · Rayleigh
optical depth ∝ λ⁻⁴ at 0.0973 for 550 nm · Ångström aerosol depth ∝ λ⁻ᵅ ·
Kasten–Young air mass · single-scattered radiance along a view ray.

The scattering model is two terms and between them they are the whole colour of
a sky: the beam reddens on the way in by `exp(−τ·X_sun)`, and the fraction
scattered toward the eye, `1 − exp(−τ·X_view)`, is blue-weighted because τ is.
Sunset is warm and the zenith is blue for the same reason, which is why one
function gives both.

### The machinery, checked against something nobody here chose

| check | measured |
|---|---|
| a 6504 K blackbody lands on the Planckian locus | x 0.3135 y 0.3237 · published (0.3135, 0.3236) |
| Wien displacement at 5778 K | 501.50 nm vs 501.52 |
| air mass at zenith / 13.5° / horizon | 1.000 · 4.21 · 37.9 |
| XYZ→linear sRGB sends D65 to equal channels | the matrix is sRGB's, not a lookalike |

That first row is the one that matters: it validates Planck + CMFs + the XYZ
transform against colour science rather than against itself.

### §9.6's requirement, met exactly

```
#4E80B4 #7BA9CE #A8CAE0 #E4DAC2 #C8D4D6 #FBE2AE #FFF1CE #FFFAEA #A9BCC7 #D6DDD4
```

All ten stops reproduced for a G-type star at 13.5°, worst channel error
**5.24e-8** against a display step of 3.04e-4 in linear light.

### And every other star moves

| property | measured |
|---|---|
| a hotter star makes a bluer zenith, monotonically | 3200 K −1.49 → 9500 K −1.83 (log r/b) |
| every stop moves the same way, so the palette stays a palette | 10 stops × 5 temperatures |
| a higher sun has a less reddened disc | 4° 0.436 → 60° 0.105 |
| the four-stop wash keeps its order under every star | 2800–12000 K, worst luminance drift 0.050 |
| haze and mist stay greyer than the sky they hang in | zenith 0.833 · haze 0.305 · mist 0.089 |
| the transfer is pure — same star, same sky | §2.3 |

The first non-solar skies this project has computed:

```
M dwarf 3200 K · zenith #3E7B7E  horizon #EFDC87  disc #FFF09B
A-type  9500 K · zenith #5B82D3  horizon #E1D9E4  disc #EBE7FF
```

### Gamut, and three ways of handling it that are worse

A 12000 K star asks for a sky bluer than sRGB holds at §9.1's painted
luminance. That is a display limit, not an error, and how it is answered decides
whether the transfer means anything:

- **Per-channel clamp** shifts hue — it answers "too blue" by making the sky
  *yellower*, which is the one thing this file must never do.
- **Exact luminance preservation, chroma compressed to fit** sounds principled
  and strangles the bright stops: at `skyHorizon`'s luminance of ~0.70 the gamut
  has almost no room for a blue hue, and an 8000 K star gave up **73.5%** of its
  chroma to hold a brightness nobody asked it to hold.
- **Nothing** lets the pale stops inflate past 1.0 and clip anyway.

What ships gives up **luminance**, and only at the top: divide by the largest
channel, which is exact — scaling all three equally leaves chromaticity
untouched. A negative channel means the hue itself is outside the gamut, which
is rare, and that case alone compresses chroma toward the grey of the same
luminance. §9.1's painted brightness is a strong preference; its hue
relationships are the thing a different star is supposed to change.

One end-to-end check had to be withdrawn, and why is worth recording: it
measured "chroma given up" by comparing each stop's saturation to the
reference's, and reported 73.5% at 8000 K — but that number was the *transfer
working*. An 8000 K horizon is genuinely near-neutral on its way from warm to
cool, and no end-to-end saturation measure can separate that from gamut loss.
The mapper is now tested as itself: in-gamut input passes through untouched, too
bright dims at exactly constant hue, an out-of-gamut hue gives up chroma at
constant luminance, and 4000 seeded colours all land inside.

## 20 · Act 2 step 5 · The bloom, rebuilt

`src/bloom.js`. §7 established that no setting of `UnrealBloomPass` can satisfy
§2.8 — its coarsest mip spans the frame, so every bright pixel contributes to
every pixel, and the pedestal that produces is structural. This replaces it with
the reference's structure: a bright pass, a **bounded** blur chain, and the
composite performed inside the print rather than as an additive pass.

Bounded is the operative word. The chain is four levels deep starting at half
resolution and the dual-filter kernel reaches about two texels per step, so the
furthest a photon travels is roughly 64 source pixels against a 2560-pixel
frame. A pixel further than that from any light receives nothing.

### Result

Cosmic scale, seed 20250601, a = 0.55, `m1=1&slab=1&comp=1&q=desktop`:

| | true `#000` | lit | halo | mean | peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ACES + `UnrealBloomPass` | 0.9% | 59.9% | 49.2% | 0.0596 | 0.900 |
| print + rebuilt bloom, 3 levels | **39.3%** | 34.7% | 27.8% | 0.0358 | 0.884 |
| print + rebuilt bloom, 4 levels | **35.8%** | 35.5% | 28.7% | 0.0361 | 0.883 |
| print + rebuilt bloom, 5 levels | 32.8% | 35.8% | 29.0% | 0.0363 | 0.883 |

Black rises monotonically as the chain shortens, which is the compact-support
claim measured rather than asserted. Peak luminance is unchanged — the cores of
the filaments keep their brightness and only the halo goes, because the halo
*was* the pedestal.

Through `tools/gate.js` at a = 0.45 the §2.8 clause now reads **43.2% exactly
`#000` against 43.0% with the dither off** — a pass, and the dither is finally
neutral rather than costing 3.2 points. Banding improved too: longest identical
run 7 px against the reference frame's 14 px.

And the frame is what §M1 asked for. Its complaint was that *"the tracers are
round dots, so filaments read as fog"*; side by side, the ACES frame is a soft
purple fog with a haze in the voids and the new one is threads of discrete
points against black, with the teal end of the divergence ramp legible per
tracer instead of averaged into the halo.

### Two bugs, both invisible in the code

**The upsample accumulated instead of blending.** An additive up chain adds each
level's full energy to the one below, so an L-level chain returns roughly L
times the light it was given: the four-level version took the web from a mean
luminance of 0.060 to **0.493** and lit 82.7% of the frame. That is not a bloom,
it is a second copy of the image. The alpha channel now carries a blend weight
and ordinary source-alpha blending does the lerp in the blender — energy in
equals energy out, and `uBloomAmt` means the same thing at every depth, which
matters because §5's quality table will set `levels` per tier.

**Karis' soft knee amplified the deep field by 88×.** The knee is a width
*around* the threshold, so it has to vanish with it — and four of AEON's six
scales ask for `threshold: 0`. With a fixed knee at threshold 0 the soft term
bottoms out at `knee/4` for every pixel however dim, so a tracer at 0.001 came
out of the bright pass at 0.088. `tune()` derives the knee from the threshold
now; at threshold 0 the whole block collapses to `w = 1`, which is the honest
reading of "bloom everything". This one alone was most of the missing black.

### Alpha, which is the other half of the change

`UnrealBloomPass` blends with `AdditiveBlending` and `transparent: true`, so it
adds its own alpha to the scene's — under §9.3 every pixel's "distance" would
become its distance plus how much it glows. The rebuilt chain renders to its own
texture and swaps nothing, so alpha survives untouched from the scene to the
print. Act 2's two halves were one change, as §16.4 predicted.

The pass order under `?m2=1` is now `render → grade → bloom → print`: the bloom
runs *after* the grade, so a world's resonance colours what glows.

### Verified

Eight flag combinations boot, compile and wire correctly, each surviving a
mid-flight resize — which reallocates the chain's targets and must re-point the
print's sampler or it reads a disposed texture:

```
default        40 shaders · UnrealBloomPass · print off
m2 cosmic      30 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
m2 surface     44 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
m2 planet      32 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
m2 blackhole   24 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
m1+m2+slab     30 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
m2 dither off  30 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
m2 levels 2    30 shaders · BloomChain · print on · bloomTex bound
```

### Still short of the ceiling, and honest about it

Turning the bloom off entirely gives 76.2% true `#000`; the rebuilt chain gives
35.8% at four levels. The remainder is light the web genuinely puts there, at
`threshold: 0` — which is what the cosmic scale asks for, and "bloom everything"
is a legitimate artistic instruction. Whether the cosmic scale should carry a
small threshold is a look decision for the §8 critic on a real GPU, not a
correctness one, and `?blevels=` exposes the other axis.

§5 is unmeasured for this change. The chain is 3 shaders against
`UnrealBloomPass`'s many and does 2·levels blits per frame rather than 11, so it
should be cheaper, but *should* is not a measurement and the desktop row has
5.7× of headroom to spend either way.

## 21 · Act 3 · The light model

`src/paint.js` — §9.2, ported from the reference's lines 622–681. Verified by
`node tools/verify.js paint`, 17 checks. Wired into the terrain behind
`?m2=1`, and separable with `?m2=1&paint=0`.

### The five ideas, and the two a PBR reflex deletes

| | |
|---|---|
| Half-Lambert wrap `ndl·0.62 + 0.46` | A 13.5° sun grazes flat ground at **ndl = 0.2334** — below the first band edge at 0.17, so plain Lambert drops the whole valley floor into shade and golden hour reads as dusk. The wrap lifts it to **0.605**, past the second edge at 0.58 |
| A three-stop ramp with **visibly banded** edges | The test measures the luminance derivative and requires **exactly two contiguous edges** at 0.170 and 0.580 with 60% of the range flat between them. It fails if anyone smooths them, which §11 says is the archetypal reflex |
| Shadows change hue, never going black | Shadowed surface comes out `[0.192, 0.264, 0.236]`, saturation 0.274, and its blue:red goes 0.596 → 1.232. Not "lit, but less" — a different colour |
| Hemispheric ambient tints rather than washes | Saturation held at 0.266 while luminance moves 11%. A surface facing the sky reads cooler (blue:red 1.232) than one facing the ground bounce (1.173) |
| Backlight rim, gated on view *and* shadow | 1.15 luma toward the sun against 0.32 away; identically zero in shadow, because a rim in shadow is a light leak |

Plus subsurface transmission, which adds **0.327** to a surface edge-on to the
sun and **0.000000** to one facing it — light coming *through*, not bouncing off.

### The lights come from the star

`starlight.js` gains §9.1's four `light` values, so they follow the spectrum the
way the sky already does. The G-type fixture reproduces `#FFD79C #9EC6E6
#AA9C64 #5C6E9E` exactly; a 3200 K star warms the sun from 1.10 to 2.07 in
log(r/b) **and its shadow from −1.16 to −0.52**. A red dwarf's shadows are not
the same violet as ours, and now they are not painted that way.

### What it looks like, honestly

Isolated by `?paint=0`, on the surface station:

| | mean | min luma | saturation |
|---|---|---|---|
| legacy lighting | 0.491 | 0.0045 | 0.293 |
| print only | 0.568 | 0.0885 | 0.195 |
| print + light model | 0.655 | 0.0885 | 0.182 |

Two things to read out of that, and only one is a defect.

**The saturation drop is mostly the print, and mostly a metric artifact.** The
frame's darkest pixel goes from 0.0045 to 0.0885 — §2.8's atmosphere floor doing
exactly what it is for. A `(max−min)/max` saturation measure falls whenever
everything is lifted off black, so it reports the lift as desaturation. The
colours did not go grey; the range compressed.

**The brightness is a real defect, and its cause is that `shadow = 1`.** There is
no shadow map at surface scale, so every fragment is fully lit; the ramp's `t`
never drops below the second band edge, and the whole frame sits on the `lit`
stop. §9.2 cannot show what it is for without a cast shadow — the band edges are
*there* (the CPU suite proves it) and nothing in the frame ever crosses them.

So the model is correct and its input is missing. The next piece of act 3 is the
shadow pass: the reference's `shadowAt()` (lines 596–618) is a five-tap lookup
with a **painterly wobble** offset specified in metres, so the shadow edge is
drawn rather than filtered. That wobble is why its shadows read as brushwork,
and it is the reason to port the shadow map rather than settle for a slope term.

Faking it from the surface normal was the alternative and it is not honest: a
slope-driven "shadow" is Lambert wearing a different name, and it would defeat
the wrap that the first row of this table exists to justify.

### Flag hygiene, corrected mid-act

`?m2=1` had come to mean three changes at once — tonemap, bloom and now
lighting — and a single flag for three changes is the same defect as no flag.
`?paint=0` separates the third, which is the only reason the table above can
attribute anything.

## 22 · Act 3, part two · The shadow pass

`src/shadow.js`. §9.2's model needs a cast shadow before its bands can show;
§21 measured that and this is the answer to it.

> **Status, corrected.** The body of this section was written while the pass was
> still broken and says so in several places. It works as of `cffcbee` — the
> shadow term tracks the sun from 0% lit to 95% across a day cycle, and two of
> the three suspected bugs turned out to be in the instrument rather than in the
> pass. That commit changed this section's heading and left the body, which then
> read as a live failure for three commits. The prose below is kept as the
> record of the search; read it as history, not as status.

### What is built

An orthographic depth pass along the sun, 480 m span, resolution from the new
`shadowRes` column in §5's quality table (1024 → 2560 by tier). Plus the
sampler: five taps in a cross, offset by two octaves of value noise **before**
they are taken, so the edge is drawn rather than filtered.

Three details are the reason to port this rather than approximate it:

- **The wobble is specified in metres** (`0.34 / span`), so a 34 cm brush stroke
  keeps its size whatever the span or the tier. In texels it would change
  character every time the quality row did.
- **The light camera snaps to its own texel grid.** Without it the map slides
  under the geometry and every shadow edge crawls — the one artefact that reads
  as *cheap* rather than as soft.
- **Bias varies with `ndl`**, `0.0022 → 0.00045`. At 13.5° the depth gradient
  across a texel is enormous and a fixed bias acnes the whole valley.

### Where it stands

Compiles on all eight flag combinations; 92/92 offline checks; the default
build is untouched. The depth map is **correct**: read back from the GPU it is
100% written with depths in [0.475, 0.509], which is sane terrain.

The *comparison* is wrong. Ground fragments come back fully shadowed, so the
frame is worse than before rather than better.

### Ruled out, with the measurement that ruled it out

| Suspect | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The pass is not running | Runs. Light matrix non-identity, camera correctly placed 760 m along the sun, 82 meshes in scene |
| Framebuffer incomplete | No completeness warning; the map is 100% written |
| `DepthTexture` attachment | Replaced with a float colour attachment and `MeshDepthMaterial`'s own packing — simpler and driver-portable. Map still correct, comparison unchanged |
| Clear colour | Was genuinely wrong — a colour attachment clears to `#000`, which reads as an occluder at depth zero. Fixed. Changed nothing, because the map is 100% covered |
| Sky dome / ocean writing into the map | Casting is opt-in now (`markCaster`, `CASTER_LAYER`). The scene holds opaque meshes at radius 20000, 9900 and 3267 which are not occluders. Correct change; did not move the numbers |
| Logarithmic depth buffer | Not enabled |

### The open question

A hand-rolled projection of known ground points disagrees with the map by
±0.07 in depth — up to ~100 m — **with the sign flipping between samples**, which
rules out a constant offset or a bias error. Either the probe is comparing
against the wrong texel (its own projection and `readRenderTargetPixels`'
y-convention are both unverified), or `heightAt()` and the rendered mesh
disagree by more than a tile's interpolation error.

The next step is to settle that one question rather than continue guessing:
render the shadow map itself to the screen and look at it. `?shdebug=2` already
outputs the probe triple — edge fade, projected depth, and what the map holds —
and the missing piece is a view of the map as an image, which is ten lines.

### What shipped anyway, and why

The module, the sampler, the quality column, `markCaster`, the clear-colour fix
and `?shdebug=1|2`. All default-off. The alternative was to hold a working
depth pass and a set of ruled-out hypotheses in a scratch directory that the
next session cannot see.

`?paint=1` also now works **without** `?m2=1`. That is not cosmetic: every
measurement of the light model until now was taken through a tonemap that lifts
shadows 2.66×, which is how a debug frame meant to show a shadow term came back
reading white.

## 23 · Act 3, part three · The landing-site solver, pointed at the real ground

§3's second ruling calls this *"the actual engineering problem the reference
sets you. Everything else is transcription."* The previous session built the
solver and the suite immediately caught it scoring the wrong field. This
session fixed that, and the fix exposed four more faults underneath it.

### The extraction, proven inert

`src/ground.js` now holds the one definition of the walkable ground —
`surface.js` composed it inline inside `_buildTerrain()` where nothing else
could evaluate it. §2.7 is written about GLSL↔JS parity at planet scale, but
it is the same rule: one definition, because two drift.

A refactor of a generation path that moves any world by a millimetre has broken
every shared URL (§2.3), so the guard is a fingerprint, not an intention. 441
samples over a ±1300 m lattice at 130 m spacing were captured from the browser
**before** the move, checksum **19546.3209**. After: **0 of 441 samples differ,
checksum identical to the digit.**

### The measurement that reset every constant

The macro field varies by **2.7 m** across the whole ±1400 m surface. The real
ground varies by **333 m**. Every threshold in the scorer had been calibrated
against a field 123× too flat, which is why `hero`, `lead` and `walls` all read
exactly zero and `lowHorizon` read exactly one — for the solved site and a
random one alike.

Re-derived from the measured distribution of each raw quantity over 288 random
(site, heading) pairs across twelve worlds, each divisor near the p75 of its
own measurement. **A term that is always 0 or always 1 is not a constraint, it
is a constant.**

### Four faults the real field exposed

| Fault | What it was | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `lead` telescoped | `Σ (sky[c] − sky[c−1])` is `sky[last] − sky[0]`. It read two columns out of twenty-one and called it a line. Harmless against 2.7 m of relief; against the real ground it pinned at 1.000 on almost every frame | Magnitude × **coherence** — how much of the total variation runs one way. Distinguishes a ridge crossing the frame from a jagged skyline with the same endpoints |
| `walls` probed three points | Three samples of a field whose finest octave is 110 m, compared in metres. A 45 m flank at 400 m subtends 6°, which is not a wall. Read 0.000 on 70% of frames | Curvature of the skyline array — the frame edges above its middle, in degrees. 21 samples instead of 3, **at no cost**. The three terms now decompose cleanly: `lowHorizon` is the skyline's mean, `lead` its linear trend, `walls` its curvature |
| `offCentre` read the wrong object | It scored where the *skyline maximum* sits, which put it in direct conflict with `lead`: a coherent cross-frame slope places its maximum against a frame edge by construction. The solver spent `offCentre` to buy `lead` and landed at exactly 0.500 on all twelve worlds against chance's 0.590 | Scores where the **hero landmark** sits — §9.7's clause is about the subject. The reference's footpath leads out of frame-left *and* its landmark sits off-axis, because they are not the same thing. Rule of thirds, asymmetric: centred is the named fault and goes to zero; frame-edge is merely weaker and tapers to half |
| The total was additive | `2.2·band + 1.6·hero + …` lets a saturated term buy out a dead one, and it did — a spectacular diagonal with no horizon and no walls | **Weighted geometric mean over a 0.06 floor.** §8 gates a frame at *"≥4 every axis, ≥4.5 mean"* — a floor per axis, *then* an average. A weighted sum cannot express that; a product can |

### And one duplicate, found while fixing them

`landing.js` carried its own copy of `frameAt`, and the two had already drifted:
at the poles `ground.js` falls back to `[1,0,0]` and `landing.js` fell back to
`cross([1,0,0], d)`. Different axes. Nobody had noticed because no test lands on
a pole. Deleted; `landing.js` re-exports `ground.js`'s.

### Where it stands

| term | random | solved | |
|---|---|---|---|
| `lowHorizon` | 0.584 | 0.701 | +12% |
| `offCentre` | 0.652 | 0.950 | +30% |
| `hero` | 0.324 | 0.363 | +4% |
| `lead` | 0.250 | 0.536 | +29% |
| `walls` | 0.056 | 0.335 | +28% |

All six beat chance; the suite is 100/100. `hero` at +4% is the weak one, and
it is weak *honestly*: the conjunctive total refuses to buy a landmark by
throwing away the horizon, and on these twelve worlds a prominent peak and a
low skyline are usually the same decision in opposite directions.

### Cost, and why the flag stays off

`scoreComposition` went 8.01 → **2.51 ms** (strided hero search — it was 71% of
the ground evaluations and prominence against a 200 m collar does not need
sampling every 1.3°; plus a per-*ground* height memo, since eight headings at
one site re-read the same near-field lattice eight times).

A full solve is **127–337 ms**, once, inside `_buildTerrain`. That is 10–28×
§5's 12 ms frame budget in the frame it lands, and §2.5 forbids covering it
with a cut. So `?solve=1` is default-off per §7.4. Flipping it waits on either
a measurement showing the hyperzoom absorbs it, or slicing the solve across
frames — a separate commit either way.

### Wired, and what it changes

Verified in-browser on `?seed=1337146641`, solver on vs off, same world:

```
(default)   yaw -0.983   sun elevation 19.6°   ← outside §9.7's 8–18° band
&solve=1    yaw -2.356   sun elevation 14.0°   ← inside, and facing the sun
            score 0.564 · lowHorizon 0.58 offCentre 0.95 hero 0.96
                          lead 0.49 walls 0.00 band 1.00
```

Azimuth only — pitch stays as art-directed. `lowHorizon` scores the skyline's
elevation *angle*, a property of the ground and not of where the camera points,
so the solver has nothing to say about tilt and should not pretend otherwise.

`_sunPhaseFacing` picks, among the phases at the solved elevation, the one that
puts the sun most nearly ahead. The sun path is a tilted circle, so an elevation
is reached twice a day on opposite sides of the sky, and §9.2 calls the
backlight rim *"the connective tissue of the whole image"* — it only fires
looking toward the sun. That choice is the difference between the light model's
best term firing and not.

### A defect the solver walked into, which it did not cause

Now that the opening frame faces the sun, every sun-facing frame shows what
sun-facing frames have always shown: hard-edged, screen-aligned bright bands
across the sky, and a washout severe enough to fail §8 axes 1 and 3.

Isolated, in order:

| control | bands present? |
|---|---|
| `?solve=1` | yes |
| solver **off**, camera turned to the same heading by hand | **yes** |
| legacy path, no `?m2=1` | **yes** |
| `uBloomAmt = 0` — bloom contribution fully removed | **yes** |

So it is pre-existing, it is not the solver, and it is not the post chain — it
is in-scene geometry, drawn before the print. Ruled out along the way:
`softDotTexture` (its corners do reach alpha 0), and the bloom pyramid (§11's
named trap, which this superficially resembles).

Not root-caused further, and deliberately not fixed here — a scene-geometry fix
does not belong inside a solver commit. It lands in **act 2**, which is §9.3's
aerial perspective: the code that renders atmosphere toward a low sun is
exactly the code being replaced next.

**Caveat: observed under SwiftShader.** Every previous surface capture on the
RTX 3060 faced away from the sun, so this needs one look on real hardware
before it is treated as confirmed.

### One false alarm, resolved

`docs/captures/shaders.json` moved 98 → 90 shaders on the `m1=1&m2=1&slab=1`
pass in this commit, which looks exactly like a silent regression — eight code
paths no longer exercised.

It is not. Re-running the gate in a clean worktree at `HEAD` **without this

## 23 · Act 2 · Aerial perspective, built

§16 designed it and §16.5 sequenced it. Steps 3, 4 and 6 landed here; step 4 is
complete for the surface scale and open for `planetscale.js`.

New module: **`src/aerial.js`** — the GLSL chunk, its CPU twin, and the three
§16.3 rulings as code rather than as prose. It follows `src/paint.js`'s shape:
one function, two implementations, and the constants shared between them by
interpolation rather than by transcription, because §2.7 names two definitions
free to drift as a trap that "will look like a rendering bug and cost a day."

### 23.1 · The one design change from §16

§9.3 says to write the fog fraction to alpha. **AEON writes `1 − fog`**, and the
reason is a difference between the two codebases rather than a disagreement
about the technique.

The reference can store fog directly because every surface in it goes through
`aerial()`. AEON has dozens of surface-scale materials — rocks, trees, ruins, a
whole city generator, three's own built-ins — and every one of them writes
`a = 1` for an opaque fragment, because that is what alpha has always meant.
Under "alpha *is* fog", each of those reads as maximally distant, and §9.4
step 5 answers by pouring its heaviest watercolour wash over the nearest tree in
the frame. The failure is silent, it is worst on the objects closest to the
camera, and it looks like a defect in the blur rather than like an unported
material.

Inverted, `a = 1` means *clear*, which is what an opaque material already means
by it. An unported surface reads as "no fog" — sharp, untouched, exactly as it
renders today — and porting one is a visible improvement rather than the removal
of a defect. The only surface that has to opt in is the sky, which writes
`a = 0` because it genuinely is at infinity.

Same information, same channel, same single trick. The encoding is chosen so
that the default is correct and the omission is safe.

### 23.2 · What the suite gained

`tools/verify.js` no longer defines `aerial()` itself. It did, which meant the
twelve checks proved a copy correct and the universe shipped something else;
the function moved to `src/aerial.js` and the suite imports it. The extraction
was inert — all twelve passed unchanged.

Fourteen checks were added, and they turned §16.3's three signed-off
recommendations into red lines:

- **§16.3a** — a temperate world reproduces `fogNear 70` / `fogFar 1700`
  exactly; thinner air sees proportionally further; the resonance's `hazeX`
  rides the same term rather than a second one; and **an airless world has no
  fog, with no special case for it.**
- **§16.3b** — the transfer reproduces §9.1's four air colours for a 5778 K star
  at 13.5° to 0.000/255, and a 3200 K star reddens them.
- **§16.3c** — `H = RT/(Mg)` gives Earth 8436 m; a one-parameter greenhouse puts
  its surface 33 K above equilibrium; and the haze layer lands on the
  reference's 260 m **by construction**, as `260 / H(Earth)`, rather than to the
  two digits §16.3 wrote it with. Haze depth then follows gravity inversely, and
  a hydrogen atmosphere holds a deeper column than a nitrogen one.

**That third airless check found a real bug.** Scaling the extinction length
with air thickness was not enough: the valley-mist pool term is added to the fog
*after* the extinction curve, so a moon with no atmosphere still grew 0.16 of
pooled mist in its valleys. Mist is condensate suspended in a boundary layer and
needs air to hang in, so it now scales with thickness too — saturating at 1, so
a temperate world is the reference's value exactly. The recommendation was
right; the implementation of it was one term short, and prose does not fail.

Suite total: **104 → 119 checks, all green.**

### 23.3 · What was measured on the frame

No GPU here (§4), so the shader was checked two ways that do not need one.

**Compiles as assembled.** 46 shaders built on the surface scale under `?m2=1`,
zero failures, zero page errors.

**The fog reaches the print.** `?fogview=1` outputs the distance the print
actually read instead of the picture — the same instrument `?shdebug=1` is for
the shadow term, and it exists because this is the one part of act 2 whose
failure mode is invisible: a chain that quietly dropped alpha still renders a
perfectly good frame, just one where step 5 softens nothing. Row means from the

| | horizon band | mid | underfoot |
|---|---|---|---|
| `?m2=1` | 0.98 | 0.17 | 0.000 |
| `?m2=1&aerial=0` | 0.000 | 0.036 | 0.000 |

Horizon-minus-underfoot: **0.922 with §9.3, 0.000 without.** The depth ramp is
the aerial function's doing and nothing else's.

### 23.4 · Open, and honest about it

- **`planetscale.js` is not converted.** It keeps its one-line `uHazeK` fog and
  writes `a = 1`, which under §23.1's encoding reads as "no fog" — so the
  streaming globe renders exactly as it did, with step 5 inert over it. An
  unfinished port rather than a regression, which is the whole point of the
  encoding. Its distances are in draw units rather than metres, so the
  conversion needs a metres-per-unit term that the surface scale does not.
- **Transparent materials dent the clarity channel.** three blends alpha for
  every `transparent: true` material, so cloud sprites, atmosphere shells and
  particles leave a mark where they overlay. Measured with `?aerial=0`: row

  rows, with one isolated pixel at 0.92. Bounded, plausibly benign — soft things
  reading as soft — and not fixed here. The correct fix is `CustomBlending` with
  `blendSrcAlpha = ZeroFactor` on those materials, which is an audit across a
  dozen modules and belongs with act 4's material pass rather than inside this
  one.
- **The pixel diff against the CPU reference** (§16.5 step 3's stated gate,
  ≥97% within 2/255) has not been run. Both implementations are generated from
  one set of constants and the suite asserts they share them, which is a
  weaker claim than the diff and is what this container can make.

### 24.4 · One act held back, on the evidence

`?paint=1` was going to ride the flip with the rest of M2. A capture stopped it.

patchwork and the grain is gone. With `?paint=0`, on the same frame, all of it
is there. `?aerial=0` changes nothing either way, which is what isolates it to
the light model rather than to the fog or the print.

The light model is not wrong. It is doing exactly what §9.2 specifies, and in
this frame that is the problem.

The three-stop ramp bands at `t = 0.17` and `t = 0.58`, and `t` is the
half-Lambert wrap `ndl·0.62 + 0.46` — which maps the entire lit hemisphere into
0.46–1.0. With the sun 24° up and a smooth dome of ground under it, every pixel
lands above the *upper* edge. One band is occupied, the ramp returns one colour,
and all the normal variation underneath is quantised away. §11 warns that a PBR
reflex deletes those band edges; it does not follow that a frame showing only
one band is the intended result.

- **§9.7's landing solver** puts the spawn sun in an 8–18° band, which is the

  stops. What feeds them today is a derivation from one base colour, marked as
  a placeholder in the shader for exactly this reason.

So it waits for its dependencies instead of shipping a regression. This is also
the strongest argument yet for debt item 3 in §24.3: the band edges need a human
to look at them, and the first frame that got looked at was one nobody would
have signed off.

**Still visible in the default frame, and not fixed here:** the rocks read as

sun-facing halo recorded in `6b7a149` is still there; it reproduces on the

## 25 · Act 4 · Four-layer triplanar materials

`src/material.js`. Behind `?mat=1`, default-off (§7.4).

What it replaces is a slope/altitude colour ramp with two noise octaves, inline
in `surface.js`'s terrain shader. It had no material identity at all: the same
lerp produced every surface, so there was nothing you could point at and name —
which §8 axis 5 asks you to do.

### 25.1 · Four layers, one blend law

`rock`, `soil`, `sward`, `rime`. The names are load-bearing rather than
decorative: a §8 scoring pass that cannot say what it is looking at has already
failed the axis, and these are the answers it is allowed to give.

The law is a **partition of unity** — four affinities, normalised — rather than
an alpha stack. An alpha stack loses the sum-to-one property the moment one
mask is edited, and the failure is a frame that quietly darkens where the masks
do not quite meet. Measured at 3.3e-16 over 35 721 points.

`rock` carries a 0.06 floor. It is there because bare stone shows through
everything, and it doubles as the reason the normalisation can never divide by
zero.

### 25.2 · The blend law is a pure function of scalars

The GLSL samples simplex noise; the CPU twin cannot without porting simplex to
JS to no purpose. So the noise is an **input** to the blend rather than
something the blend reaches for. Both sides run the same algebra over whatever
their noise gave them, `tools/verify.js` tests the algebra, and the suite
asserts the GLSL carries the same constants. §2.7's parity rule is about the
height field and this does not touch it.

### 25.3 · The gate's tiling clause, measured

*"At 1.68 m eye height, no visible tiling within 40 m in any biome."*

Three statistics were tried and two were wrong, which is worth recording
because both were wrong in the same way:

1. **Peak autocorrelation** read 0.916 at a one-metre lag and called it a tile.
   That is not tiling, it is *smoothness* — adjacent points on any continuous
   field are correlated, and ground that failed this would be noise.
2. **Autocorrelation rebound** read 1.235 at 24 m. Also not tiling: 24 m is the
   meso octave's feature size, and any band-limited noise puts a bump there. A
   spectral statistic cannot tell a field that repeats every 24 m from one that
   merely *has* 24 m features.
3. **Direct self-match** is the gate's own sentence: is there a shift that maps
   the material onto itself? Mean squared difference under a shift, normalised
   so 0 is a perfect tile and 1 is uncorrelated.

Result, over three 33 m patches: **the closest self-match at any shift between
5 m and 40 m is 0.544.** Under 5 m it reaches 0.028, and that is reported but
not gated — at half a metre the field matches itself almost exactly, which is
what *continuous* means. Two patches only read as a repeat once they are far
enough apart to be seen as two.

Triplanar is the mechanism, not a style choice. A single planar projection
smears on anything steep, and those smears are the most visible repetition a
landscape has — the clause fails on a cliff face long before it fails on flat
ground.

### 25.4 · It does **not** un-hold `?paint=1`, and here is the measurement

§24.4 held §9.2 back on the theory that its three stops were three points on
one line through one colour, and that real material stops would fix it. Half of
that was right. The stops are real now — the suite measures each material's
shade→lit hue travel at 36–140° of hue angle, all in the right direction — and
the frame is **still flat**.

The reason is not the colours on either side of the band edges. It is that the
terrain's own ramp coordinate never reaches them.

| sun elevation | t range over the terrain | bands occupied |
|---|---|---|
| 24° (default spawn) | 0.646 – 0.748 | `lit` only, **100%** |
| 18° (what `?solve=1` chose) | above the edge | `lit` only |
| 13.5° (the reference's) | 0.535 – 0.644 | mid 35% · lit 65% |
| 8° | 0.487 – 0.578 | mid 96% |

`t` is the half-Lambert wrap `ndl·0.62 + 0.46`, and the band edges sit 0.41
apart at 0.17 and 0.58. **The terrain's t spans 0.10.** So at any sun angle the
ground can occupy at most a quarter of one band, and whether an edge is visible
at all depends entirely on where that 0.10-wide sliver happens to land.

Two consequences, and the second is the larger one:

- §9.7's golden-hour band is not a mood, it is a **precondition** — the section
  says so and this is that sentence as a number. But `?solve=1` picked 18°, the
  top of its 8–18° window, which is already too high. If the solver is what
  puts the sun where §9.2 needs it, its window needs narrowing to roughly
  8–14° on this landform.
- More fundamentally, **the spawn landform is too smooth for the ramp.** The
  reference is a valley with real slope variety; a 0.10-wide t is a dome. Even
  perfectly positioned, only a sliver of the ramp is ever exercised.

**A proposal, needing sign-off (§7.2) rather than a commit.** Place the band
edges relative to the terrain's measured t distribution instead of fixing them
at 0.17 and 0.58. §9.2's edges are the art direction and §11 is right that
deleting them is the archetypal error — but the reference's *values* are a
measurement of the reference's valley, exactly as §16.3 found for the fog's
extinction lengths, and the same ruling ought to apply: port the function that
produced them, not the numbers. That keeps the bands discrete and visible while
guaranteeing they land where there is ground to band.

Until that is decided, `?paint=1` stays held and act 4 ships on its own merits.

## 26 · Act 5 · The sea

`src/ocean.js`. Behind `?sea=1`, default-off (§7.4).

What it replaces is two crossed sine waves. A sine is symmetric about its own
mean and the sea is not: it has crests and troughs of different shapes, and
that asymmetry is where every readable feature of water lives.

- **Twelve trochoidal waves** on a Pierson–Moskowitz spectrum, spread around
  the wind vector, advancing at deep-water phase speeds from `ω² = gk`. `g` is
  the world's own `GM/R²`, so a low-gravity sea genuinely runs longer and
  slower rather than being told to.
- **Steepness capped on the summed Jacobian**, not per wave — self-intersection
  is a property of the sum, and capping the terms individually neither prevents
  it nor bounds it.
- **Beer–Lambert extinction** at `[0.45, 0.075, 0.021] m⁻¹`, quantised into five
  discrete depth bands per the reference's river rather than run as a
  continuous ramp.
- **Whitecaps from two terms** — the Jacobian fold where the surface genuinely
  overturns, or wind-driven crest coverage — band-limited against the mesh's
  Nyquist so foam does not alias into sparkle at distance.

One expectation was wrong and the code was right: the summed steepness came out
at 0.104 against an expected 0.5–1.0. It is correct. `H_s ∝ U²` *and*
`λ_peak ∝ U²`, so steepness is roughly invariant in wind speed; the test was
asserting a number nobody had derived.

And one misdiagnosis, recorded because the process failure is the interesting
part. Pale diagonal streaks over the water read as foam, and the foam model was
rewritten three times chasing them — Nyquist band-limiting, then patchiness,
then per-pixel crest evaluation. None of it moved the streaks. Bisecting the
scene found them in a single capture: **foreground grass on a 205 m coastal
hill, silhouetted against a sea far below.** All three changes were kept because
each is a real improvement, but none was fixing what it was claimed to fix. The
lesson is cheap and general: bisect the scene before the shader.

## 27 · Act 6 · Far ridges as pure silhouette

`src/horizon.js`. Behind `?ridge=1`, default-off (§7.4). §M2's last clause:
*"Distant terrain: the reference renders far ridges as pure haze, pure shape —
silhouette only, no detail. Do the same; cheaper **and** better."*

### 27.1 · The one place this must not be a transcription

The reference builds its ridges from noise — `buildRidgeBand()` sums five
octaves of `abs(noise2)` around the azimuth and calls the result a mountain. It
is entitled to. Its valley is 2400 m of authored ground and there is no terrain
beyond it to be faithful to; the ridges are a backdrop because a backdrop is all
that exists.

AEON's height field is defined at every point on the planet. `heightAt(x, z)`
answers at 40 km as readily as at 4 m. Inventing a horizon here would be drawing
a different mountain than the one you would reach if you walked toward it —
§8 axis 8 failed on the largest object in the frame, and the same class of
defect §2.7 exists to prevent one scale down.

So the crest line is **measured, not generated**. For each azimuth a ray marches
the world's own height field and records the maximum elevation angle, which is
what a skyline *is*: the upper envelope of the terrain along the line of sight,
not the height at some chosen radius. The curtain is then drawn at a convenient
radius with its height set to reproduce that angle exactly.

> A billboard at any distance carries the true silhouette of terrain at any
> other distance, provided it preserves the elevation angle.

That is the whole trick, and it is why this is cheaper without being a lie. The
suite pins it to float64: worst reprojection error 8.7e-9 over 442 columns.

**The module introduces no entropy at all** — no RNG, no seed, no hash, no
clock. The horizon is a reading of the terrain, so it is deterministic for
exactly the reason the terrain is (§2.3), and the suite asserts the source
contains none of the four.

### 27.2 · "Cheaper" is a measurement, not an assertion

`surface.js` builds three nested terrain rings. Ring 2 spans 2212 m to 7000 m
(9899 m at the corners) at 194 m per quad, running the **full** terrain fragment
shader over the entire horizon band: fbm octaves, four-layer triplanar under
`?mat=1`, a shadow-map lookup under `?paint=1`.

Under §9.3 it is almost entirely invisible. Measured, on a temperate world:

| where | clarity (1 − fog) |
|---|---|
| ring 2's inner edge, 2212 m | **1.55 %** |
| ring 2's corner, 9899 m | **1.9 × 10⁻¹¹ %** |

It contributes between nothing and 1.55 % of its own colour, and 100 % of its
cost. `saturationRadius()` is that judgement computed rather than asserted: it
inverts §9.3's own curve for the radius at which fog reaches 0.995, and the ring
is retired when — and only when — that radius falls inside the ring's corner.
A thin-atmosphere world sees 17 km and **keeps** its ring. An airless one has no
extinction at all and keeps it too. All three fall out of the same two lines
with no branch, which is the check that the parameterisation is right rather
than fitted.

Four full bands are 1760 triangles against ring 2's 9400. The fragment shader
they carry evaluates **no noise and samples no texture**, against the terrain
fragment's 20 noise call sites and 6 shadow-map samples under `?mat=1&paint=1`
— several of those triplanar and therefore three lookups each. That, rather
than a ratio nobody measured, is why it is cheap: there is nothing in it to be
expensive. The measurement itself is 15.8k–19.6k height evaluations against the
28.2k that meshing the finest ring already spends.

### 27.3 · Two limits, both real; two decisions, both computed

- **`saturationRadius(params, crestY, hazeH)`** — where §9.3's own fog reaches
  0.995. Crest height matters enormously: a crest 400 m up sits above most of
  the boundary-layer haze and sees roughly twice as far as the valley floor it
  rises from, which is why a ridge line is visible when the ground beneath it
  is not.
- **`geometricHorizon(Reff, eyeH, crestH)`** — `√(2Re) + √(2Rh)`, both heights
  measured above the **local surface**, and `Reff = Rworld · 0.34` because
  `ground.js` renders the curvature drop with that exaggeration and the horizon
  has to agree with the ground it stands on rather than with the ephemeris.
  Checked against the exact tangent length `√(2Rh + h²)` at three radii.

Then two decisions that had to be separated:

- **The ring is retired** ⇒ a band is *required*. The ground now stops in
  mid-air, and something has to close the gap between its edge and the sky,
  even where the air saturates before the curtain.
- **The ring is kept** ⇒ a band is *optional*, added only where there is
  genuinely visible ground beyond the ring's corner. Measured on a mountainous
  world at `atmo = 0.25`: one band from 10.1 km to 16.2 km, 0.91° of relief,
  61 % fog at the crest. On a small airless moon the geometric horizon is
  1793 m, inside the ring's own corner, and the honest answer is **no geometry
  at all**.

### 27.4 · Occlusion, not the air, decides how many are drawn

`bandPlan()` lays out a geometric progression from the extinction curve, but
the air cannot know what hides what. A band that rises nowhere above everything
nearer than it is invisible, and the first version drew four curtains and showed
one on a world whose nearest ridge was also its tallest.

So bands are pruned against a **running occlusion**: the retained ground's own
silhouette, raised by each kept band's crest in turn. The same running value
places each band's foot, which stopped the outer bands being drawn fourteen
degrees tall so their feet could reach a valley floor three nearer curtains
already covered.

Pinned from both sides on synthetic ground, so the rule does not depend on which
fixture happens to have the right shape: terrain that climbs with distance keeps
4 of 4 planned bands; a dome falling away collapses 4 to 1.

### 27.5 · The foot, and the guarantee

The reference gives each band one negative constant (`y: -40 … -260`). Here the
foot is per column and derived: `min(front, crest) − BASE_DROP`, where `front`
is what actually stands in front of that column. Two properties follow as
assertions rather than hopes:

- the foot is never above what hides it, so no sky can show between the ground's
  edge and the curtain — **provable**, because the terrain profile is continuous
  from the eye outward, so a ray at a shallower angle than a crest must strike
  that crest's near flank first. The suite casts the rays rather than restating
  the argument: 32/32 occluded.
- the foot is never above the crest, so the strip never inverts where a near
  hill stands taller than everything behind it.

`BASE_DROP = 0.05` is checked against the quantity it actually has to beat —
how far the true occlusion dips below the straight edge drawn between two
sampled columns. Measured worst dip: 0.0123.

And the case where the ring is retired *and* every band is pruned is checked
too, because those are separate decisions that can combine into "the ground
stops at 2310 m and there is nothing behind it". It is safe for the same
continuity reason, and a bowl fixture casts 24 rays to confirm no leak.

### 27.6 · Shading: two cues, both readings of something real

There is no normal here and deliberately no attempt to fake one. §M2 asks for
pure shape; a shading term invented for a surface with no geometry is the exact
"PBR instinct" §11 warns about, and it would put lighting detail on the one
object in the frame whose entire job is to have none.

- **The sunward arc** is warmer, because those slopes face the sun — a
  ring-scale fact that survives having no normals. Guarded on the sun's
  azimuthal length: at zenith the sun has no azimuth, and a `normalize()` of a
  zero vector reaches the bloom pyramid as a solid block (§11).
- **The crest is lighter than the foot**, and §9.3's height falloff produces
  that on its own from the true heights the vertices carry. The reference paints
  the gradient by hand (`mix(0.72, 0.30, t)`); here it is derived, so it changes
  correctly when the air does.

The **albedo is area-averaged**, not the terrain's own. A mountain six
kilometres away is the average of rock, vegetation, snow, shadowed gully and lit
flank over several hectares — darker and much less saturated than any
ingredient. Painting a ridge in the terrain's albedo is the commonest way a
distant hill ends up looking like a cardboard cutout of a near one.

Both vertices carry the **true** distance and height of the terrain they stand
for, as attributes, corrected in the fragment shader for wherever the camera has
walked since. Feeding §9.3 the curtain's own position instead would grade a
12 km peak as though it stood at 4 km — the fog and the geometry disagreeing
about the same pixel.

### 27.7 · What is owed

- **Anchoring, and the case that will force the fix.** The bands are anchored at
  the spawn, because §9.7's opening frame is composed from the spawn and the
  silhouette is the largest thing in it. Walking away costs a bearing error, and
  at 0.99 haze a bearing error is not a visible quantity.

  **Altitude is the sharper case, and it is not covered.** `F` puts the walker
  in flight, and from 944 m the real skyline moves both outward and down while
  the curtain stays where a 1.8 m eye put it. The `d-vantage-ridge-on` capture
  is therefore *outside the design envelope*: it shows that the geometry builds,
  grades correctly through §9.3, and replaces ring 2's 194 m-quad mottle with a
  clean haze-graded silhouette — but it is **not** evidence about accuracy at
  that altitude, and it should not be read as any.

  The fix is an incremental re-march on a frame budget — the march is
  per-azimuth and embarrassingly resumable, so N columns per frame retires the
  error without the 80 ms hitch a rebuild would cost. It belongs with M4's
  camera work rather than here, because that is where the flying eye is
  actually specified. Until then `?ridge=1` is honest on foot and approximate
  in flight, and this paragraph is the record of which.
- **Build cost.** 87–166 ms in node for the march, on top of a terrain build
  that already spends 44k height evaluations. §5's load budget is 1.2 s to first
  pixel and this is a load-time cost, but it is the specific line item a GPU run
  has to price.
- **Eyes.** Whether a 0.13°-relief band on a flat world reads as a horizon or as
  a seam is not a CPU question. Routed via `docs/GPU-RUN.md` with the rest.

## 28 · §2.7's parity test, run for the first time, and what it found

`node tools/pixeldiff.js --suite terrain`.

§2.7 is one of the nine invariants, and it is the only one that specifies its
own test in numbers:

> **GLSL↔JS height-field parity.** `src/terrain.js` is an exact port of the
> orbital height field. Change the noise in GLSL, port it in the same commit,
> with a numeric parity test over 10⁴ samples (max abs error < 1e-4 of planet
> radius). Break this and the coast you saw from space stops being the coast you
> walk.

§11 lists the failure mode by name: *"`terrain.js` drift. Will look like a
rendering bug and cost a day."*

That test had never been run. `tools/verify.js` says why, in its own words at
the head of the check that stands in for it: *"Not a parity test — that needs a
GPU."* It needed a harness that could hand a GLSL chunk to a real driver and
compare it against a CPU twin, which is what act 2 built for §9.3. So the test
is now four lines of glue on top of `pixeldiff.js`, and it fails.

### The result

10⁴ Fibonacci-sphere directions × five worlds, against the *shipped*
`src/terrain.js`:

| seed | max abs Δh | as a fraction of R | over tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.317 | 0.7894 | 4.55e-3 | 5947/10000 — **59.5%** |
| 1 | 0.7946 | 4.58e-3 | 58.5% |
| 7.77 | 0.8324 | 4.80e-3 | 58.1% |
| 42.5 | 0.7969 | 4.60e-3 | 55.4% |
| 913.25 | 0.7771 | 4.48e-3 | 61.2% |

Tolerance is 1.733e-2 in `h`, which is §2.7's 1e-4 of planet radius converted
through `planetscale.js`'s own numbers (`R = 2600`, `amp` up to 15). So the
worst sample is **46× the budget**, and the majority of every world is outside
it. The four seeds under 100 are in `system.js`'s actual range
(`noiseSeed: pr.float(0, 100)`); the fifth is out of range and behaves no
differently, so seed magnitude is not the driver.

This is not a cosmetic difference. `planetHeight` is what `ground.js` builds the
walkable ground from, what `landing.js` picks a landing site with, and what
`city.js` and `hydrology.js` place settlements and rivers by. The orbital shader
uses the same field to decide where a coastline is *drawn*. §2.7's sentence is
the exact failure: the coast you see from space is not the coast you walk.

### Two things ruled out before believing it

**The harness.** The shader was made to return its input unchanged and the
readback compared to the array that was uploaded: **worst |gpu − input| = 0
across all 10 000 samples**, both endpoints exact. The index mapping, the 2D
tiling and the readback row order are all correct. (The first version used a
10 000-wide texture and returned "incomplete framebuffer", which is what
`MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE` looks like when nothing says so.)

**The algebra.** `src/terrain.js` was read against `NOISE_GLSL` line by line —
the skew constants, the `step`/`min`/`max` ranking, `i1`/`i2`, the three-deep
permute chain and its per-component arguments, `ns`, the `b0.xzyw`/`sh.xxyy`
gradient fold, the `taylorInvSqrt` placement, and the `m⁴` weighting. They
match. The port is correct.

### The cause, and the control that proves it

**`src/terrain.js` is an exact port of the algebra and not of the arithmetic.**
It computes in float64; the shader computes in float32.

For most maths that is a rounding error. Not for this one. Ashima's `mod289` is
`x − floor(x · (1/289)) · 289`, and by the third `permute` the argument is
around 10⁷, where float32's spacing and the rounding of `1/289` together are
enough that the two languages **pick different integers**. When they do, the
result moves by 289 — which selects an entirely different gradient. A
discontinuity, so no tolerance argument reaches it.

The control settles it. `--f32` runs the identical algebra with `Math.fround`
at every step, which is what the GPU does:

| seed | max abs Δh, float64 | max abs Δh, float32 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.317 | 0.7894 | **2.554e-5** |
| 1 | 0.7946 | **5.326e-5** |
| 7.77 | 0.8324 | **3.811e-4** |

0 of 10 000 samples outside tolerance, on every world, with 45× to 700× of
margin. The disagreement is entirely arithmetic width.

### What this means, and what it does not

The fix direction is not symmetric. The GPU is what draws the planet, so the
**CPU has to match the GPU** and not the other way round: `terrain.js` should
compute in float32.

That is a change to a generation path, so §2.3 applies with full force — every
world moves, every shared URL lands somewhere else, and the `ground` suite's
golden checksums are invalidated by design rather than by accident. It is
exactly the kind of change that needs a human to say yes.

**And a second finding, which is worse and is not fixed by any of this.** The
shader's own evaluation is float32 through a `floor`, so it is
*driver-dependent*. Two GPUs that round `x · (1/289)` differently at 10⁷ will
draw different coastlines for the same seed. §2.3 promises "same seed + same
code = same universe on every machine, forever", and that promise currently
rests on every GPU agreeing about a discontinuity. This run cannot detect it —
one machine, one driver. It needs the same test on a second GPU, and
`docs/GPU-RUN.md` is where that instruction belongs.

### The second finding, decided on one machine after all — and it changes the fix

The paragraph above says one machine cannot detect whether two GPUs would
disagree. That is wrong, and `node tools/pixeldiff.js --suite fragility` is why.
The question is not *"do these two drivers agree"* — it is *"is this field's
float32 evaluation **fragile**"*. A fragile one will be disagreed about by any
two implementations that round differently anywhere, and drivers differ for a
dozen ordinary reasons: a contracted multiply-add, the order a compiler
associates a sum, whether `floor` is an instruction or a conversion.

So perturb by one unit in the last place — smaller than any legitimate
difference between two drivers — and measure how far the output moves.

**The first version of this test measured the wrong thing and said "stable".**
It perturbed the *input direction*, a quantity of magnitude 1, and found nothing:
0 of 40 000 samples moved past tolerance, worst 3.79e-4. That is a real result
and it is not the question. The discontinuity is not at the input. It is inside
`mod289`, whose argument reaches about 10⁷ by the third `permute`, where one ULP
is nearly 1.0 — and two drivers differ at *intermediates*, never at inputs.

Perturbing there, by one ULP, in the one operation drivers actually disagree
about:

| seed | samples past tolerance | worst Δh | as a fraction of R |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.317 | **9941/10000** | 0.932 | 5.38e-3 |
| 1 | 9948/10000 | 0.974 | 5.62e-3 |
| 7.77 | 9953/10000 | 0.950 | 5.48e-3 |
| 42.5 | 9947/10000 | 0.954 | 5.50e-3 |

**99.5% of the planet moves past §2.7's budget for a one-ULP difference in an
intermediate.** The float32 evaluation is not merely imprecise, it is balanced on
a discontinuity, and cross-driver agreement is luck rather than a property.

Three consequences, in order of how much they cost:

1. **§2.3 is exposed on real hardware today**, independent of the JS port. Two
   GPUs that contract one multiply-add differently draw different coastlines for
   the same seed. Nothing in this repo has ever tested that, and the promise —
   *"same seed + same code = same universe on every machine, forever"* — is
   currently resting on drivers happening to agree.
2. **`fround`-ing `src/terrain.js` would match one driver, not fix the problem.**
   The recommendation this section originally carried is withdrawn.
3. The real fix is the one this section called "a much larger change", and it is
   smaller than it sounded.

### The fix: compute the permutation in integers

Ashima's `permute` is `mod289(((x·34)+10)·x)` over values that are *always
integers* in 0…288. It is integer arithmetic written in floats because GLSL 1.00
had no usable integer type. WebGL2 compiles `#version 300 es`, which does: the
whole chain fits in `int` — the largest intermediate is 288·34+10 = 9802 times
288, about 2.8×10⁶, against an int32 ceiling of 2.1×10⁹.

Integer arithmetic has no rounding, so there is no floor to flip and no driver
freedom to exercise. It is exact on every GPU that compiles the shader, and
`src/terrain.js` mirrors it with `|0` arithmetic for parity that is exact rather
than approximate. §2.7's test would then pass by construction instead of by
tolerance.

It is not free. The float32 version is *approximating* an integer computation
and sometimes getting it wrong, so making it exact moves every world — once,
deliberately, to the values the algorithm always intended — and then never
again. §2.3 says that is a human's call, and this time the argument for paying
it is that the alternative is a promise the project cannot keep.

Recommended, in order:

1. Port `permute`/`mod289` to integer arithmetic in `NOISE_GLSL`, mirror it in
   `src/terrain.js`, and re-run `--suite terrain`: it should return exact
   equality rather than a tolerance.
2. Re-run `--suite fragility`: the intermediate test should report stable,
   because there is no longer an intermediate to perturb.
3. Re-take the `ground` goldens in the same commit, with the world shift
   measured and stated.
4. Then the two-GPU run in `docs/GPU-RUN.md` becomes a confirmation rather than
   a discovery.

### 28.1 · Correction: the permutation was not the fault

The section above recommends porting `permute`/`mod289` to integers. **That is
wrong, and it is wrong in a way worth keeping on the page**, because the
reasoning that produced it was sound and the conclusion still did not survive
contact with a measurement.

The integer permutation is built, behind `?intnoise=1`, and with *only* that
change the shader's output is **bit-identical** to the float path — the same four
probe values to six decimal places. `node tools/pixeldiff.js --suite terrain
--int` returns numbers indistinguishable from the float run, seed for seed and
sample for sample. The permutation was never the fault. The argument for it —
large intermediates, a `floor`, a discontinuity — was correct about the
mechanism and wrong about the location.

### 28.2 · The actual fault, which is in the gradient table

Four probe points, one shader, three implementations:

| point | float chunk | integer chunk | `terrain.js`, float64 |
|---|---|---|---|
| (4.79, 3.84, 12.04) | 0.849920 | **−0.907243** | −0.907244 |
| (1.5, 2.5, 3.5) | 0.593943 | 0.593943 | 0.593943 |
| (100.25, 200.5, 300.75) | 0.043806 | 0.046652 | 0.061230 |
| (400.1, −200.3, 88.8) | 0.185405 | 0.185405 | 0.670681 |

The first point is the whole finding. It sits at coordinates near 12, where no
precision argument applies at all, and it disagreed by **1.76** — more than the
noise's own range. Dissecting it: the skew agreed to 3.8e-7, the cell index
agreed exactly, `x0` agreed exactly, the corner ranking agreed exactly. The
divergence is downstream of all of them.

It is in the gradient table. With `x_`, `y_` integers in 0…6,

```
x = x_·(2/7) + (0.5/7 − 1) = (4·x_ − 13) / 14        exactly
h = 1 − |x| − |y|          = (14 − |4x_−13| − |4y_−13|) / 14
```

and **seven of the forty-nine cells have `h` exactly zero** — (0,3) (1,2) (2,1)
(3,0) (4,6) (5,5) (6,4). `sh = −step(h, 0.0)` is then decided entirely by
rounding noise, and the two precisions land on opposite sides of **all seven**:

```
  x_=0 y_=3   h64 −1.110e-16 → sh −1     h32 +1.192e-7 → sh 0
  x_=1 y_=2   h64 −1.110e-16 → sh −1     h32 +1.192e-7 → sh 0
  x_=2 y_=1   h64 −1.110e-16 → sh −1     h32 +1.192e-7 → sh 0
  x_=3 y_=0   h64 −1.110e-16 → sh −1     h32 +1.192e-7 → sh 0
  x_=4 y_=6   h64 +2.220e-16 → sh 0      h32 −2.384e-7 → sh −1
  x_=5 y_=5   h64 +4.441e-16 → sh 0      h32 −1.192e-7 → sh −1
  x_=6 y_=4   h64 +2.220e-16 → sh 0      h32 −2.384e-7 → sh −1
```

When `sh` flips, both gradient components shift by one — a different gradient
vector, so the corner's contribution changes outright rather than slightly.
That is 14% of the table, it does not depend on coordinate magnitude, and it
explains a disagreement at coordinate 12 that nothing else could.

**Neither implementation is right.** Ashima's own semantics are unambiguous:
`step(edge, x)` returns 1 when `x >= edge`, so `step(0.0, 0.0)` is 1 and `sh`
must be −1 for all seven. float64 gets four of them, float32 gets the other
three, and the integer form gets all seven — on every machine, because there is
nothing left to round. The test is one line and exact:

```glsl
ivec4 hI = ivec4(14) - abs(4 * xI - 13) - abs(4 * yI - 13);
vec4  sh = -step(vec4(hI), vec4(0.0));
```

Verified against the rational form on all 49 cells. With it, the first probe
point moves from 0.849920 to −0.907243 against float64's −0.907244.

### 28.3 · What is still open, stated as open

The gradient fix does not close everything. Probe 3 moves toward float64 without
reaching it and probe 4 does not move at all, both at coordinates in the
hundreds. So **there is at least one more divergence, and it is
magnitude-dependent** where this one was not — which points back at the terms
the first analysis suspected: the cell index `floor(v + dot(v, C.yyy))` at
coordinates near 500, or `x0 = v − i + t` as a difference of two large numbers.

The honest status: one fault found and fixed exactly, one or more remaining,
`?intnoise=1` carrying both integer changes and default-off, and §2.7's gate
still failing. What must not happen is this section being read as finished
because it now contains a root cause. It contains *a* root cause.

The recommendation in §28 is withdrawn and replaced by: keep going until
`--suite terrain` is exact, then re-take the goldens once, then confirm on a
second GPU. Not the other way round.

### 28.4 · Narrowing the remainder

The two probe points that still disagree, dissected the same way the first one
was:

| | (100.25, 200.5, 300.75) | (400.1, −200.3, 88.8) |
|---|---|---|
| skew | identical | differs 4.6e-6 |
| cell index | **same** | **same** |
| corner ranking | **same** | **same** |
| `x0` worst component | differs 1.0e-5 | differs 6.1e-6 |
| **output** | 0.046652 vs 0.061230 | 0.185405 vs 0.670681 |

A 6.1e-6 difference in `x0` producing a 0.485 difference in output is a gain of
eighty thousand. That is another discontinuity, not amplification — and the
usual suspects are eliminated by the table above.

It is also not the two faults already characterised. The permutation is exact in
this path (integer), and neither point's four corners land on any of the seven
zero-`h` cells — which is why probe 4's integer and float results are identical
to six decimals.

So the remaining divergence is **downstream of the ranking and outside both
known faults**, which leaves the final assembly: `m = max(0.5 − dot(x0,x0), 0)`
and the `m⁴` weighting, or `src/terrain.js`'s port of the `b0.xzyw` / `sh.xxyy`
gradient fold. The `m` clamp is the one remaining place a small input change can
produce a large output change — a corner crossing the 0.5 support radius
switches from contributing to contributing nothing — though that should be
continuous *at* the boundary, since `m` goes to zero there. If it is not
continuous in practice, that is the next thing to measure.

Recorded at this depth rather than left as "still fails" because the next person
should not have to re-derive that the skew, the cell, the ranking, the
permutation and the gradient table are all cleared.

### 28.5 · Closed: it was the gradient table all along, on both sides

§28.3 said the gradient fix left "at least one more divergence, and it is
magnitude-dependent". That was wrong, and the way it was wrong is worth keeping.

Every intermediate of both implementations, traced side by side on the two
probe points that still disagreed:

| | cell index | permutation `P` | `xg`, `yg` | `d2`, `m` | **`sh`** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (400.1, −200.3, 88.8) | same | same | same | agree to 5 dp | **differs, corner 1** |
| (100.25, 200.5, 300.75) | same | same | same | agree to 5 dp | **differs, corners 0 and 2** |

The differing corners land on cells **(6,4)**, **(5,5)** and **(1,2)** — three of
the seven where `h` is exactly zero. There was never a second fault. §28.3
inferred "these points do not hit the seven cells" from the integer chunk
leaving them unchanged, and that inference was invalid: the integer chunk
*agreeing with the float chunk* is what you get when the integer answer happens
to equal the float one, which is exactly the case on cells where float32 already
guessed right.

**Which exposes the real shape of it: `terrain.js` was wrong too.** On the seven
zero-`h` cells, float64 reads four as `<= 0` and float32 reads the other three,
and Ashima's semantics say all seven are `-1`. Each side was wrong about a
different subset, so the shader and the port disagreed *because both were wrong,
in complementary ways*. Fixing only one side would have moved the disagreement
without removing it.

So `src/terrain.js` takes the same integer test, threaded through `snoise`,
`fbm`, `ridged` and `planetHeight` as an `exact` argument defaulting to the
`?intnoise=1` flag — an argument rather than a module constant, so `tools/` can
measure both paths without a browser.

### 28.6 · §2.7, measured on both sides fixed

`node tools/pixeldiff.js --suite terrain --int`, 10⁴ samples per world:

| seed | before | after | over tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.317 | 0.7894 | **1.582e-5** | 0/10000 |
| 1 | 0.7946 | **6.999e-5** | 0/10000 |
| 7.77 | 0.8324 | **3.175e-4** | 0/10000 |
| 42.5 | 0.7969 | **1.695e-3** | 0/10000 |
| *913.25* | 0.7771 | 3.784e-2 | 467/10000 |

**§2.7 passes on every seed a world can actually have.** `system.js` draws
`noiseSeed: pr.float(0, 100)`, so the first four are in range and the fifth is
not — 913.25 was added to this suite as an out-of-range stress case, and it is
the only one still over. Its residual is ordinary float32 accumulation at nine
times the coordinate magnitude, not a discontinuity: the worst in-range figure,
1.695e-3, sits ten times inside §2.7's budget of 1.733e-2, where before the
worst was forty-six times outside it.

That stress row stays in the suite and stays failing. A seed generator that ever
widens its range would be told immediately, which is worth more than a green
line.

### 28.7 · And the fragility question dissolves

`--suite fragility --int` no longer runs its intermediate test, and the reason is
the answer rather than a dodge: the intermediate it perturbs is `mod289`'s
argument, and under the integer permutation there is no float intermediate there
to perturb. No rounding, no freedom for two drivers to differ.

What stays float is the skew and `x0`, and the input-perturbation test measures
exactly those: **0 of 40 000 samples move past tolerance, worst 3.79e-4.** So the
§2.3 exposure §28 raised — two GPUs drawing different coastlines for one seed —
is closed by construction on the integer path, and measured stable on what
remains of the float one.

`?intnoise=1` is still default-off. Flipping it moves every world once,
deliberately, to the values Ashima's own semantics always specified, and that is
a human's call under §2.3. But the case for it is now a measurement rather than
an argument, and the `ground` goldens can be re-taken in the same commit with
the shift stated.

## 30 · Should `?wash=1`'s default flip? — and the measurement that answers a bigger question

§27.11 showed the depth ordering inverts back once `?airmat=1` gives the
foreground a real distance, which removes the blocker §25 put on the wash. So
the flip is decidable. §8 says the critic is adversarial by design, and it warns
that a local metric can miss a global one — §25 and §27.11 both measure mean
`|∇luma|` inside small regions, which says nothing about the frame as a whole.

So: the same frame, six ways, seed 1337146641 at 1280×720 with `&dt=16.667`
pinned, HUD strips excluded from the statistics.

### The number nobody had taken

| build | mean | **sd** | min | max | range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| legacy (no `m2`) | 156.9 | **39.0** | 33.3 | 242.0 | 208.7 |
| `m2=1&paint=0` — print and bloom, no light model | 148.2 | **34.1** | 48.3 | 223.0 | 174.7 |
| `m2=1` | 157.9 | **27.1** | 51.1 | 223.0 | 171.9 |
| `m2=1&airmat=1` | 158.7 | 25.1 | 51.1 | 223.0 | 171.9 |
| `m2=1&wash=1` | 158.5 | 24.2 | 58.8 | 220.9 | 162.0 |
| `m2=1&airmat=1&wash=1` | 158.4 | **24.6** | 51.7 | 220.9 | 169.2 |

**Global contrast falls monotonically as M2 comes on: 39.0 → 24.6, a 37% loss.**
Not one step — three, and the wash is the smallest of them:

```
  the print and the rebuilt bloom     39.0 → 34.1     −13%
  §9.2's light model                  34.1 → 27.1     −21%   ← the largest
  airmat + wash                       27.1 → 24.6      −9%
```

And nothing in any M2 build reaches black or white: every frame lives inside
51–223, with 90% of it between 112 and 207.

### What that does and does not prove

It does **not** prove a defect, and this section will not claim one from a single
frame. Three of those steps are supposed to compress:

- §9.2's half-Lambert wrap exists to lift a grazed valley floor from `ndl ≈ 0.23`
  to 0.60. Compressing the shading range is its stated job, and §9.2's own note
  says plain Lambert would make golden hour read as dusk.
- §9.4's lift raises blacks by construction (§2.8, §3 row 1) — in an atmosphere
  *nothing may reach pure black*, and a floor near 34/255 is what the lift's
  `(0.017, 0.021, 0.036)` predicts.
- The reference itself is a low-contrast painterly image. A high standard
  deviation is not the goal.

The ceiling is the more suspicious half, and it is partly explained too: 223/255
is 0.875 through the print's curve, which corresponds to a scene luminance near
3.0. This frame faces away from a +20° sun on a 240 K overcast ocean world and
contains no sun disc and no strong specular, so there may simply be nothing
brighter in it. Legacy reached 242 partly because the old additive bloom *added*
energy where the rebuilt one has compact support and does not.

What it does prove is that **the trend was never measured**, that it is monotone,
and that it is large. Three separate changes each took a fifth to a tenth of the
frame's contrast and each was signed off on its own evidence. §8's sixth axis
asks about clipping and hue families and never asks about range, which is how
this got through.

### The recommendation: flip `airmat`, hold `wash`

Split, because they buy different things at a similar price.

**`?airmat=1` should flip.** It costs 2.0 of standard deviation (7%) and closes
a §8 axis-3 defect that predates all of act 2: `scene.fog` is unset, so ~120
objects were not fogged at all and a conifer at 600 m stood at full contrast
against hazed ground behind it. Fixing a depth cue that is *absent* is worth
more than 7% of a range that is already generous in the midtones. The colossus's
own contrast falling from 6.958 to 3.525 is not a loss — it is the hero landmark
finally sitting in air.

**`?wash=1` should not flip yet.** It costs 2.9 (9%), the largest of the two, to
buy a softening whose §9.4 justification is real but whose visible benefit on
this frame is subtle — and it spends that contrast on an image that is already
the flattest in the chain. Turning on more compression at sd 24.6, against
legacy's 39.0, is the wrong direction to move until the trend above is
understood.

That is not a verdict on §9.4 step 5, which is correct, verified and ported
faithfully. It is a verdict on *sequencing*: the wash should land after somebody
has looked at where 37% of the contrast went, not before.

### What to measure next, and it is not the wash

Take this table across the whole capture set rather than one frame — six scales,
three tiers, and at least one warm world with the sun in frame, which is the
case that would show whether the 223 ceiling is this world or the pipeline. If
the ceiling holds at 223 with a sun disc in shot, the print is capping
highlights and that is a defect. If it reaches 250, this frame was simply dim
and only the standard deviation is the story.

`tools/tone.js` is that measurement, committed. It takes a `--builds` sweep and
reports mean, standard deviation, saturation, floor, ceiling and a sixteen-bin
histogram per build, with the HUD strips masked — they are a fixed population of
near-white pixels in every build and would flatter the range identically
everywhere, hiding the thing being measured. It flags any sweep that loses more
than a tenth of the standard deviation.

One instrument note, because it produced a beautifully confident wrong answer:
the first version read the canvas in-page with `drawImage` and reported a table
of **zeroes for every build**. A WebGL canvas created without
`preserveDrawingBuffer` is cleared once composited, so there was nothing to
read. It measures a decoded `page.screenshot()` now. Same shape of mistake as
§28's first fragility test — an instrument pointed at the wrong thing does not
report an error, it reports a number.

    node tools/tone.js --builds "m2=1&airmat=0,m2=1,m2=1&wash=1"

      m2=1&airmat=0   157.9  27.1  0.306   51  223  172
      m2=1            158.7  25.1  0.299   51  223  172
      m2=1&wash=1     158.5  24.6  0.302   52  221  169
